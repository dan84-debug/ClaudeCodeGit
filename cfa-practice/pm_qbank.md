# PA1 — Jones / Bragg (Attribution Effectiveness, Risk Attribution, Sortino, Capture)

*Full vignette: Appendix → PA1*

### Q1 — Effective Performance Attribution [PA-1]

**Context (PA1):**
Jones (senior adviser, Federalist Investors) asks Bragg (junior analyst) to describe an effective attribution process. Bragg's monthly evaluation of a fixed-income manager covers measurement, attribution and appraisal.
- **Response 1:** attribution draws conclusions regarding the quality of a portfolio manager's investment decisions.
- **Response 2:** attribution should help explain how performance was achieved by breaking apart the return or risk into different explanatory components.

**Question:**
Which of Bragg’s responses regarding effective performance attribution is correct?

A. Only Response 1
B. Only Response 2
C. Both Response 1 and Response 2

**Correct Answer:** B

**Explanation:**

- **A is wrong.** Response 1 is wrong: drawing conclusions about the quality of the manager's decisions is **appraisal**, not attribution. *(My reading; the key explains only why B is right.)*
- **B is correct.** Attribution explains *how* performance was achieved by breaking return or risk into components. Effective attribution accounts for all return/risk exposure, reflects the investment decision process, quantifies the manager's active decisions and fully explains excess return/risk.
- **C is wrong.** Response 1 is wrong, so 'both' can't be right.

**Key Principle:**
> Measurement = what; attribution = how; appraisal = judging the quality of the manager's decisions.

### Q2 — Risk Attribution for a Top-Down Fixed-Income Manager [PA-2]

**Context (PA1):**
Bragg notes the fixed-income manager has strong views about the effects of macroeconomic factors on credit markets and follows a top-down investment process. The portfolio is managed against a benchmark.

**Question:**
The most appropriate risk attribution approach for the fixed-income manager is to:

A. decompose historical returns into a top-down factor framework.
B. evaluate the marginal contribution to total risk for each position.
C. attribute tracking risk to relative allocation and selection decisions.

**Correct Answer:** C

**Explanation:**

- **A is wrong.** Decomposing historical returns into a top-down factor framework is return-based factor attribution, not risk attribution relative to the benchmark. *(My reading; the key doesn't discuss A and B.)*
- **B is wrong.** Marginal contribution to total risk by position is an absolute-risk, bottom-up measure; the portfolio is managed against a benchmark. *(My reading.)*
- **C is correct.** The portfolio is benchmark-relative, so use relative-risk attribution. For a top-down process, attribute **tracking risk** to allocation and selection decisions relative to the benchmark.

**Key Principle:**
> Benchmarked manager → relative risk (tracking risk) attribution; top-down → allocation and selection decisions.

### Q4 — Interpreting the Capture Ratios [PA-4]

**Correct Answer:** B

**Explanation:**

- **A is wrong.** A ratio above 1 is convex, not concave.

### Q5 — Maximum Drawdown and Drawdown Duration [PA-5]

**Context (PA1):**
Exhibit 1 (see Q3): maximum drawdown −24.00%; drawdown duration 4 months; 10-year period.

**Question:**
The maximum drawdown and drawdown duration in Exhibit 1 indicate that:

A. the portfolio recovered quickly from its maximum loss.
B. over the 10-year period, the average maximum loss was −24.00%.
C. a significant loss once persisted for four months before the portfolio began to recover.

**Correct Answer:** A

**Explanation:**

- **A is correct.** Maximum drawdown is the cumulative peak-to-trough loss over a continuous period. Drawdown duration is the total time from the start of the drawdown until the cumulative drawdown recovers to zero (drawdown phase + recovery phase). A 24% maximum loss recovered in a total of four months, within a 10-year window, is a quick recovery.
- **B is wrong.** Maximum drawdown is the single largest peak-to-trough loss, not an average. *(My reading; the key doesn't discuss B and C.)*
- **C is wrong.** Four months is the *total* duration from the start of the drawdown to full recovery, not the time before recovery began. *(My reading.)*

**Key Principle:**
> Max drawdown = worst peak-to-trough loss. Drawdown duration = start to full recovery (decline + recovery).

- **A is wrong.** It must measure all active decisions, not only allocation. For a bottom-up stock picker who ignores sector benchmark weights, sector allocation effects are just a by-product, not a decision.
- **B is wrong.** Holdings-based is generally more accurate than returns-based, but the key is reflecting the manager's actual process; either can do so. The choice depends on data availability and quality, client reporting needs and the complexity of the decision process.
- **C is correct.** An effective attribution **quantifies the impact of the manager's active investment decisions**. If it doesn't reflect the decision-making process, it has little value to the manager or client.

**Key Principle:**
> Attribution must mirror the manager's investment process and quantify active decisions.

### Q42 — Transactions-Based vs Holdings-Based Attribution (2) [PA-42]

Compared to holdings-based attribution, transactions-based attribution:

B. provides a less accurate analysis but is easier to implement.
C. uses both portfolio holdings data and transactions data to provide the most accurate analysis.

**Correct Answer:** C

**Explanation:**

- **A is wrong.** Transactions-based **includes** transaction costs: weights and returns reflect all transactions in the period.
- **B is wrong.** Reverse: it is *more* accurate but harder to implement.
- **C is correct.** Uses **both holdings and transactions data** to provide the most accurate analysis.

**Key Principle:**
> Transactions-based = holdings + trades + costs; most accurate, most work.

### Q43 — BHB Allocation Effect Sign [PA-43]

**Context:**
Manager overweights a sector. Sector return 4%; overall benchmark return 6%. Model: Brinson–Hood–Beebower (BHB).

**Question:**
An equity portfolio manager is overweight a sector relative to the benchmark. The sector return is 4% while the overall benchmark return is 6%. Using the Brinson–Hood–Beebower (BHB) model for return attribution analysis, the allocation effect for this sector will be:

A. negative.
B. zero.
C. positive.

**Correct Answer:** C

**Explanation:**

- **A is wrong.** That would be the Brinson–Fachler answer (4% < 6%), not BHB.
- **B is wrong.** An overweight times a nonzero return is not zero.
- **C is correct.** **Positive.** BHB allocation A_i = (w_i − W_i) × B_i uses the sector's benchmark return (not return minus total benchmark). An overweight in a sector with a positive return always gives a positive allocation effect.

**Key Principle:**
> BHB allocation = (w − W) × B_i. Brinson–Fachler allocation = (w − W) × (B_i − B).

### Q49 — Misfit Active Return [PA-49]

**Context:**
Manager return 11.0%; normal portfolio return 8.0%; investor benchmark return 13.0%.

**Question:**
An analyst gathers the following information about a portfolio:

- **Manager return:** 11.0%
- **Normal portfolio return:** 8.0%
- **Investor benchmark return:** 13.0%

The portfolio manager's misfit active return is:

A. −5.0%.
B. −2.0%.
C. 3.0%.

**Correct Answer:** A

**Explanation:**

- **A is correct.** **−5.0%.** Misfit active return = normal portfolio return − investor benchmark = 8.0% − 13.0% = **−5.0%**.
- **B is wrong.** −2.0% is the investor's (mismeasured) active return: 11.0% − 13.0% = true active + misfit = 3.0% + (−5.0%).
- **C is wrong.** 3.0% is the **true active return**: manager − normal portfolio = 11.0% − 8.0%.

**Key Principle:**
> Mismeasured active = true active (manager − normal) + misfit (normal − investor benchmark).

### Q51 — Asymmetry from Upside/Downside Returns [PA-51]

**Context:**
Geometric averages: manager upside 0.55%; benchmark upside 0.70%; manager downside −0.55%; benchmark downside −0.60%.

**Question:**
An analyst gathers the following geometric average performance of an investment portfolio:

- **Manager upside return:** 0.55%
- **Benchmark upside return:** 0.70%
- **Manager downside return:** –0.55%
- **Benchmark downside return:** –0.60%

The asymmetry of the capture ratio is:

A. negative.
B. zero.
C. positive.

**Correct Answer:** A

**Explanation:**

- **A is correct.** **Negative.** Capture ratio = (0.55%/0.70%) / (−0.55%/−0.60%) = 0.7857/0.9167 = **0.857 ≈ 86%** (reproduced). Below 1 → negative asymmetry (concave).
- **B is wrong.** Zero asymmetry would need a ratio of exactly 1; equal manager up and down magnitudes don't make it symmetric versus the benchmark.
- **C is wrong.** Positive would need a ratio above 1.

**Key Principle:**
> CR = UC/DC. CR < 1 → negative asymmetry (like negative convexity/gamma).

### Q52 — Sharpe Ratio [PA-52]

**Question:**
Which of the following statements about the Sharpe ratio is most accurate?

A. It measures the additional return for bearing risk above the market return
B. It is commonly used on an ex ante basis to evaluate risk-adjusted returns
C. It assumes investors are indifferent between upside and downside volatility

**Correct Answer:** C

**Explanation:**

- **A is wrong.** It measures excess return over the **risk-free rate**, not the market return.
- **B is wrong.** It is commonly used **ex post** (historical).
- **C is correct.** It assumes investors are **indifferent between upside and downside volatility** (standard deviation counts both).

**Key Principle:**
> Sharpe: (R − Rf)/σ, ex post, treats up and down volatility the same.

### Q53 — Evidence of Manager Skill [PA-53]

**Question:**
Which of the following would be most indicative of manager skill?

A. An information ratio of 0.43
B. An annual portfolio return 8.1% above benchmark
C. An annual portfolio volatility 3.3% below benchmark

**Correct Answer:** A

**Explanation:**

- **A is correct.** **An information ratio of 0.43.** It accounts for return versus the benchmark and for the volatility of that active return, so it can indicate skill, especially over longer periods.
- **B is wrong.** An 8.1% excess return on its own isn't necessarily skill (it ignores the risk taken).
- **C is wrong.** Volatility 3.3% below the benchmark on its own isn't necessarily skill.

**Key Principle:**
> Information ratio = active return / tracking error: risk-adjusted evidence of skill.

### Q55 — Type I and Type II Errors for Connell [PB-4] (Essay)

**Context (PB1):**
A year later, Connell reviews one-year results vs the benchmark (assume performance is mean reverting): **Zeta** in line; **Eta** underperforms; **Theta** outperforms.
- **Alternative 1:** keep the inheritance in the money market account to avoid the Eta fund.
- **Alternative 2:** place the inheritance in the Theta fund.

**Question:**
Identify the type of error Connell is at risk of committing and its associated cost for each alternative. Justify your selection.

**Model Answer / Explanation:**

**Alternative 1 → Type II error.** Avoiding Eta because of recent underperformance, with performance reverting to the mean, risks failing to hire/retain a skilled manager. A Type II error is failing to detect a true relationship; its cost is the **opportunity cost** of not hiring Eta and seeing its performance improve.

**Alternative 2 → Type I error.** Choosing Theta because of recent outperformance, with performance reverting to the mean, risks hiring a manager who then underperforms expectations. The cost is **explicit and relatively easy to measure**.

The goal is to avoid deciding on short-term performance (trend following) and to look for behavioral biases in manager evaluation.

**Key Principle:**
> Type I = hire/retain a manager who underperforms (explicit cost). Type II = fail to hire/retain a skilled manager (opportunity cost).

### Q56 — When Style Analysis Is Most Useful [PB-5] (Essay)

> Style analysis works best with liquid, frequently priced holdings.

### Q86 — Bonus-Style Fees and Risk Estimation [PB-33] (Essay)

**Context (PB5):**
Porter: *"More managers are applying a bonus structure in which the manager is not fully exposed to the downside but is fully exposed to the upside."* Smith: *"Circue's current market view is that there are increasing risks to the downside."*

**Question:**
Discuss how Smith's stated expectation would be reflected in estimated portfolio risk under the fee structure identified by Porter.

**Model Answer / Explanation:**

It would be reflected in a **misestimation (underestimate) of portfolio risk**. Performance-based fees convert symmetrical gross active-return distributions into asymmetrical net active-return distributions: variability is reduced on the upside but not the downside. A single standard deviation computed on a series of active returns above and below the base fee can therefore **underestimate downside risk**. **Key Principle:**
> Bonus-style fees → asymmetric net returns → standard deviation understates downside risk.

### Q87 — Net Active Return Under a Sharing Fee Schedule [PB-34] (Essay)

**Question:**
Calculate the August composite return by asset-weighting the portfolio returns using beginning-of-period values.

|---|---|---|

### Q159 — Modified Dietz for April [GP-39]

**Context:**
Beginning value $95,000 (1 April); ending $101,650 (30 April); $5,000 inflow at end of day 10 April (not large).

**Question:**
A portfolio has a beginning value of $95,000 on 1 April and an ending value of $101,650 on 30 April. It received a $5,000 cash inflow at the end of the day on 10 April that was not considered a large cash flow. Using the Modified Dietz method, the portfolio's return for April is closest to:

A. 1.68%.
B. 1.74%.
C. 1.80%.

**Correct Answer:** A

**Explanation:**

- **A is correct.** **1.68%.** (101,650 − 95,000 − 5,000) ÷ [95,000 + 5,000 × 20/30] = 1,650 ÷ 98,333.33 = 1.678%.
- **B is wrong.** 1.74% = 1,650 ÷ 95,000, i.e., cash flow not weighted in the denominator (1.737%).
- **C is wrong.** 1.80% = 1,650 ÷ (95,000 − 3,333), i.e., weighted flow subtracted (1.800%).

**Key Principle:**
> Modified Dietz weight = days flow was in portfolio ÷ days in period (end-of-day flow on day 10 → 20/30).

### Q160 — Non-Fee-Paying Portfolios [GP-40]

**Question:**
A firm manages fee-paying and non-fee-paying portfolios using a similar strategy. According to the GIPS standards, the non-fee-paying portfolios:

A. must be excluded from the composite.
B. must be included in a separate composite from the fee-paying portfolios.
C. may be included in the composite, but additional disclosures may be required.

**Correct Answer:** C

**Explanation:**

- **A is wrong.** They need not be excluded.
- **B is wrong.** No separate composite is required.
- **C is correct.** **May be included, but additional disclosures may be required.** Non-fee-paying discretionary segregated accounts and pooled funds may be included.

**Key Principle:**
> Non-fee-paying discretionary portfolios: may be in composites, with disclosure.

### Q161 — Next Step in the Valuation Hierarchy [GP-41]

**Question:**
In the GIPS valuation hierarchy, if quoted prices for similar investments in active markets are not available or appropriate, which valuation input is recommended as the next to use?

A. The most recent actual valuation for the investment
B. Quoted prices for similar investments in markets that are not active
C. Market-based inputs, other than quoted prices, that are observable for the investment

**Correct Answer:** B

**Explanation:**

- **A is wrong.** Most recent actual valuation is not the next step.
- **B is correct.** **Quoted prices for similar investments in markets that are not active.**
- **C is wrong.** Observable market-based inputs come only after inactive-market quotes.

**Key Principle:**
> Order: identical (active) → similar (active) → inactive-market quotes → observable inputs → subjective inputs.

### Q162 — Seed Money Portfolios [GP-42]

**Question:**
An investment management firm creates a new strategy and manages portfolios with its own assets using this strategy. These seed money portfolios:

A. are not permitted to be included in the firm composites.
B. can only be included in appropriate firm composites after the strategy has an established track record of at least three years.
C. can be included from inception in appropriate firm composites, subject to any presentation and reporting requirements related to the inclusion of non-fee-paying portfolios in composites.

**Correct Answer:** C

**Explanation:**

- **A is wrong.** Internally funded portfolios aren't barred.
- **B is wrong.** There's no minimum track record for inclusion.
- **C is correct.** **Can be included from inception**, subject to the presentation and reporting requirements for non-fee-paying portfolios.

**Key Principle:**
> Seed capital portfolios can be in composites from inception (non-fee-paying rules apply).

### Q163 — Too Few Composites vs Too Many [GP-43]

**Question:**
Compared to creating multiple specific composites, grouping diverse portfolios together into a single composite may:

A. lead to unnecessary costs.
B. compromise client confidentiality.
C. produce a wide dispersion of portfolio returns.

**Correct Answer:** C

**Explanation:**

- **A is wrong.** Unnecessary costs come from *too many* composites.
- **B is wrong.** Compromising client confidentiality comes from too many (narrow) composites.
- **C is correct.** **Produce a wide dispersion of portfolio returns.** Too few composites can group diverse portfolios in an overly broad composite.

**Key Principle:**
> Too few composites → wide dispersion; too many → cost, tiny composites, confidentiality risk.

### Q164 — Terminated Portfolio Cut-Off (August) [GP-44]

**Context:**
Monthly composite returns. Notified 15 August of termination effective 31 August, told to stop trading immediately.

**Question:**
An investment management firm that calculates composites using monthly portfolio returns is informed on 15 August that its management contract is being terminated effective 31 August and is instructed to stop trading immediately. According to the GIPS standards, the firm should include the portfolio in its composite through:

A. 31 July.
B. 15 August.
C. 31 August.

**Correct Answer:** A

**Explanation:**

- **A is correct.** **31 July.** Terminated portfolios are included through the last full measurement period prior to notification.
- **B is wrong.** 15 August is not a month-end.
- **C is wrong.** 31 August is the contract end.

**Key Principle:**
> Terminated portfolio included through last full period before discretion ended.

### Q165 — Years of Performance Required [GP-45]

**Context:**
A firm has claimed GIPS compliance for 12 years.

**Question:**
A firm has claimed compliance with the GIPS standards for the past 12 years. To meet the minimum requirements of the GIPS standards, the firm is required to present GIPS-compliant annual performance for:

A. 5 years.
B. 10 years.
C. 12 years.

**Correct Answer:** B

**Explanation:**

- **A is wrong.** Five years is the starting minimum, not the ongoing requirement.
- **B is correct.** **10 years.** At least 5 years initially, extended each year until 10 are presented.
- **C is wrong.** Only the most recent 10 years are required.

**Key Principle:**
> Minimum: 5 years, building to 10. More than 10 not required.

### Q166 — Verification Statement That Is Most Accurate [GP-46]

**Question:**
Which of the following statements about verification of GIPS compliance is most accurate?

A. A verification report may be issued only with respect to the entire firm
B. Verification provides assurance that specific composite returns are correctly calculated and presented
C. GIPS standards recommend that firms have a verification performed by the firm's internal audit department

**Correct Answer:** A

**Explanation:**

- **A is correct.** **A verification report may be issued only for the entire firm.**
- **B is wrong.** Verification doesn't give assurance that specific composite returns are correct.
- **C is wrong.** GIPS recommends a qualified, independent third party, not internal audit.

**Key Principle:**
> Verification: firm-wide, third-party, no composite-level assurance.

## Formulas Used in This Bank

**Terms:** R_P = portfolio return · R_B = benchmark return · R_F = risk-free rate · MAR = minimum acceptable (target) return · σ = standard deviation · w = portfolio weight · W = benchmark weight · R_i, B_i = portfolio and benchmark return in sector i · B = total benchmark return.

**Risk-adjusted measures**
> Sharpe = (R_P − R_F) / σ_P (PA-35, PA-52)
> Sortino = (R_P − MAR) / target semideviation; so target semideviation = (R_P − MAR) / Sortino (PA-3, PA-24, PA-35)
> Information ratio = (R_P − R_B) / tracking error (standard deviation of active return) (PA-53)
> Return due to style = benchmark return − market index return; active return = R_P − R_B (PA-34)

**Capture ratios and drawdown**
> Upside capture = manager geometric mean ÷ benchmark geometric mean, over periods when the benchmark is up
> Downside capture = same ratio over periods when the benchmark is down
> Capture ratio (CR) = upside capture ÷ downside capture; so downside capture = upside capture ÷ CR (PA-37, PB-64)
> CR > 1: positive asymmetry (convex); CR < 1: negative asymmetry (concave); CR = 1: symmetric (PA-4, PA-38, PB-17)
> Maximum drawdown = largest peak-to-trough decline; drawdown duration = time to recover (PA-5, PB-19)

**Return attribution (Brinson family)**
> Brinson–Fachler allocation = (w_i − W_i) × (B_i − B) (PA-10, PA-21, PA-27)
> BHB allocation = (w_i − W_i) × B_i (PA-17, PA-43)
> Selection = W_i × (R_i − B_i) (PA-26)
> Interaction = (w_i − W_i) × (R_i − B_i) (PA-17, PA-32)
> Active return = allocation + selection + interaction (summed across sectors)

**Benchmark misfit**
> Misfit active return = normal portfolio return − investor benchmark return
> Mismeasured active return = (manager − normal portfolio) + misfit (PA-49)

**Fixed-income exposure decomposition**
> Duration effect = return impact of a parallel yield shift; curve effect = impact of the change in curve shape (PA-44)

**Performance-based fees**
> Billed fee = base fee + sharing % × (return − base fee) when return exceeds the base (PB-34, PB-46, PB-48, PB-50)
> Net active return = gross active return − billed fee (PB-34, PB-46)
> Bonus-style fee: higher of (1) base or (2) base + sharing of positive performance, optionally capped at the maximum annual fee (PB-40, PB-41, PB-48)
> Symmetric fee: base + sharing of performance, positive or negative (PB-39, PB-54)
> High-water mark: reduce the current gain by prior losses before computing sharing (PB-55)

**GIPS calculations**
> Modified Dietz = (EMV − BMV − CF) ÷ (BMV + Σ CFᵢ × wᵢ); wᵢ = (days remaining after flow) ÷ (days in period) (GP-8, GP-39)
> Time-weighted return with large cash flows: revalue at the flow, compute each sub-period return, link geometrically (GP-4)
> Composite return (beginning-value weighted) = Σ (portfolio BMV ÷ composite BMV) × portfolio return (GP-9)
> Composite return (BMV + flows weighted) = weights from the Modified Dietz denominators (GP-10)
> Required GIPS Report history: at least 5 years, extended each year to 10 (GP-22, GP-45)
